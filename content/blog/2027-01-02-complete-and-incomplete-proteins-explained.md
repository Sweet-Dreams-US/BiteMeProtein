You may have heard that some foods have "complete" protein and others have "incomplete" protein. The terms sound like a grade, as if one kind is good and the other is lacking. That is not what they mean. This post explains what the words describe, which foods fall on each side, and how a normal, varied day of eating covers the gap. It is general information, not advice about your own diet.

## Protein is built from amino acids

Protein in food is made of smaller pieces called amino acids. The Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health describes protein as made from "twenty-plus basic building blocks called amino acids." Your body strings them together in different orders to make new protein.

Some of those amino acids your body can make for itself. Others it cannot. MedlinePlus, from the U.S. National Library of Medicine, says "Essential amino acids cannot be made by the body. They must be supplied by food." Harvard names nine of them: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine.

Those nine are the reason the words complete and incomplete exist.

## What complete and incomplete mean

A complete protein is a food that supplies all of the essential amino acids. An incomplete protein is a food that is low in, or missing, one or more of them. It is a description of the food, not a warning about it.

Harvard says animal foods such as meat, poultry, fish, eggs and dairy are generally complete proteins. It says plant foods such as fruits, vegetables, grains, nuts and seeds "often lack one or more essential amino acid." It also names quinoa and chia seeds as examples of plant foods that are complete proteins.

So the rule of thumb is that animal foods are usually complete, and most plant foods are not on their own, with a few exceptions.

:::note Worth knowing
MedlinePlus states that "You do not need to eat animal products to get all the protein you need in your diet." Plant foods such as soy, beans, legumes, nut butters and some grains all provide amino acids.
:::

## Why an incomplete protein is not a problem

An incomplete protein still gives you amino acids. It just does not give you all nine essential ones in one food. Different plant foods tend to be short on different amino acids, so eating a range of them fills in what any single food leaves out.

Harvard does not tell people to pair particular plant proteins at the same meal. Its advice for people who avoid animal foods is that they "can eat a variety of protein-containing plant foods each day." Its bottom line is to "mix up your sources so no 'essential' components of protein are missing."

That is a much easier standard than matching foods meal by meal. You are looking at variety over a day, not a perfect plate every time you eat.

## The whole day matters more than one meal

MedlinePlus makes the same point from a different angle. The essential amino acids do not have to show up at every meal. In its words, "The balance over the whole day is more important."

If you have eggs at breakfast, a bean dish at lunch and something with whole grains at dinner, the mix across the day does the work. You do not need to calculate it.

## Examples of how foods can fit together

Here are some ways the sources' advice looks in daily eating. These are examples of variety, not a prescription.

A day that includes eggs or dairy already includes complete proteins, since Harvard lists both among the generally complete animal foods.

A day built on plant foods can include beans or other legumes, soy foods and grains. Harvard's examples for people who avoid animal foods come from legumes, nuts and seeds, and whole grains, and it says to eat a variety of them each day.

Quinoa and chia seeds are plant foods Harvard names as complete proteins, so they are easy additions to a plant-based day.

Spreading these foods across breakfast, lunch, dinner and snacks is one simple way to get that variety. Any meal or snack can add to the mix.

## What to take from all this

Complete and incomplete are labels for the amino acids in a single food. They are not a ranking of which foods are worth eating. Most people eat a mix of foods across a day, and that mix is what covers the nine essential amino acids.

If you are curious about your own protein needs, a doctor or a registered dietitian can look at your situation. A general article cannot.

## The short version

Protein is made of amino acids, and nine of them must come from food. A complete protein has all nine. An incomplete protein is short on one or more. Animal foods are generally complete, many plant foods are not on their own, and quinoa and chia are plant exceptions. Eating a variety of foods through the day covers what you need, which is why MedlinePlus says the balance over the whole day matters more than any single meal.
