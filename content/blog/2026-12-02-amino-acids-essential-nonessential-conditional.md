Protein is a word you see often when you read about food, but it covers a lot of ground. Protein is made of smaller pieces called amino acids, and those pieces are sorted into three groups. This post explains the groups in plain terms, so the next time you read about protein you know what the terms mean.

## What an amino acid is

MedlinePlus, the health information site of the U.S. National Library of Medicine, explains that the protein in food is broken down into parts called amino acids during digestion. Your body then uses those parts for its own needs.

The Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health describes protein as made from "twenty-plus" basic building blocks called amino acids. It also notes that the body does not store amino acids the way it stores some other nutrients. Some it makes from scratch, and others it makes by modifying amino acids it already has.

That is the basic idea. Food protein is taken apart, and the body builds new protein from the pieces. The groups below describe where the pieces come from.

## Essential amino acids

Essential amino acids are the ones the body cannot make. MedlinePlus puts it this way: essential amino acids cannot be made by the body, and they must be supplied by food.

Harvard's Nutrition Source says there are nine essential amino acids, and that they must come from food. The word "essential" does not mean the others are unimportant. It describes where they come from. These nine have to arrive through what you eat.

## Nonessential amino acids

Nonessential amino acids are the ones the body can make for itself. MedlinePlus says they are made by the body from essential amino acids or in the normal breakdown of proteins.

This group has a confusing name. Nonessential does not mean unnecessary. The body needs these amino acids too. The label means only that you do not have to get them directly from food, because the body can produce them.

## Conditional amino acids

MedlinePlus lists a third group called conditional amino acids. It says these are needed in times of illness and stress. The page does not say more than that, so this post will not either. If you want to know how any of this applies to your own body, the right person to ask is a doctor or a registered dietitian.

:::note Worth knowing
MedlinePlus sorts amino acids into three groups: essential, which must be supplied by food; nonessential, which the body makes; and conditional, which are needed in times of illness and stress.
:::

## Complete and incomplete protein

The Nutrition Source uses two more terms. A complete protein, it says, contains all the types of amino acids needed to make new protein. An incomplete protein lacks one or more of the nine essential amino acids.

The same source says animal-based foods tend to be good sources of complete protein, while plant-based foods often lack one or more essential amino acids. It names quinoa and chia seeds as examples of plant foods that are complete proteins.

Notice the wording: "tend to" and "often." These are general patterns, not rules for every food. A label that says a food is high in protein does not tell you which amino acids it contains, so the terms above are a way to read about food, not a way to judge a single item.

## The balance over the day

A common worry is that every meal has to contain every essential amino acid. MedlinePlus says otherwise. It states that essential amino acids do not need to be eaten at every meal, and that the balance over the whole day is more important.

For people who eat mostly plant foods, the Nutrition Source gives a practical suggestion. It advises people who avoid animal foods to eat a variety of protein-containing plant foods each day, so they get all the amino acids needed to make new protein. It also says that if most of your protein comes from plants, you should mix up your sources so no essential component is missing.

MedlinePlus adds that you do not need to eat animal products to get all the protein you need in your diet.

So the short version is variety. Instead of tracking each meal, look at what you eat across a day and across a week.

## Where amino acids come from in food

MedlinePlus says amino acids come from animal sources such as meat, milk, fish and eggs, and from plant sources such as soy, beans and nut butters. That list shows how wide the range is. Both animal and plant foods supply amino acids, and a varied day of eating draws on more than one source.

## What to take from this

Protein is made of amino acids. Nine of them are essential and must come from food. Others are made by the body, and some are conditional. Neither source asks you to build each meal around a checklist. The advice is to look at the whole day and to vary your protein sources, especially if most of your protein comes from plants.
