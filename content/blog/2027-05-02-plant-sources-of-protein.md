Protein is not only found in meat, eggs and dairy. Beans, lentils, soy foods, nuts, seeds and whole grains all provide it, and many people eat them every day without thinking of them as protein foods. This article goes through the main plant sources, what a serving gives you, and what two trusted references say about putting them together.

## How much protein people need

MedlinePlus, from the U.S. National Library of Medicine, says protein should make up 10% to 35% of total calorie needs for healthy adults. Each gram of protein supplies 4 calories. As an example, on a 2,000-calorie diet, 100 grams of protein is 400 calories, or 20% of daily calories.

Harvard's Nutrition Source gives a body-weight figure. The National Academy of Medicine recommends a minimum of 0.8 grams of protein for every kilogram of body weight per day. That works out to just over 7 grams for every 20 pounds of body weight.

:::note Worth knowing
MedlinePlus states that you do not need to eat animal products to get all the protein you need.
:::

## Beans, lentils and soy

MedlinePlus lists pinto, black and kidney beans, lentils, split peas and garbanzo beans among the plant sources of protein. It says that half a cup (65 grams) of cooked beans or lentils gives about the protein found in one ounce of meat. It also says that one ounce of most protein-rich foods contains about 7 grams of protein.

Harvard gives a figure for lentils: one cup of cooked lentils has 18 grams of protein and 15 grams of fiber. The Nutrition Source adds that lentils have virtually no saturated fat or sodium.

Harvard reports that nuts and legumes are linked to a lower risk of type 2 diabetes, and that about one daily serving of beans, chickpeas, lentils or peas may increase fullness.

Soy, tofu, tempeh and other soy protein products are on MedlinePlus's list of plant sources. A quarter cup (60 milliliters) of tofu provides about the protein in one ounce of meat, according to the same page. Harvard also names soy foods, including tofu, tempeh and edamame, as protein sources, though it gives no gram amounts for them.

## Nuts and seeds

MedlinePlus names almonds, hazelnuts, peanuts, peanut butter, sunflower seeds and walnuts. It also notes that nuts and seeds are high in fat, so portion size matters.

Harvard gives a number for almonds: one ounce of dry roasted, unsalted almonds has 6 grams of protein and 3.1 grams of fiber. That is a single one-ounce serving, which is the portion size the page uses.

## Whole grains

MedlinePlus says whole grains contain more protein than refined or white products. It lists wheat germ and quinoa among the grains that provide protein. Harvard notes that quinoa and chia seeds are complete plant proteins.

Whole grains are one more place protein can come from during the day.

## Why variety matters

Protein is built from amino acids. Harvard explains that nine of them are essential, meaning the body cannot make them and they must come from food: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine.

Harvard also says plant foods often lack one or more of the essential amino acids, so people who eat mostly plants should combine a variety of protein sources. MedlinePlus adds that essential amino acids do not need to be eaten at every meal, and that the balance over the whole day matters more.

In practice, that means you do not have to plan every plate. Looking at the whole day is enough.

## The protein package

Harvard makes a point that is easy to miss: the source of protein, which it calls the protein package, likely affects health, not only the amount. Plant proteins come with more unsaturated fat and no cholesterol, and Harvard says this helps explain their link to lower heart disease risk.

The Nutrition Source also reports that diets higher in plant protein from legumes, soy and nuts lowered total and LDL cholesterol compared with diets built around red meat. It reports that nuts were associated with less weight gain.

These are findings about eating patterns reported by Harvard, not a promise about any one person or one food.

## Reading a serving size

The figures above come from different references and use different serving sizes, so they are not a ranking. MedlinePlus measures in ounces and half cups, and Harvard's table uses one cup of cooked lentils and one ounce of almonds. When you compare foods, compare the same amount.

MedlinePlus also gives a way to work out your own share. Protein supplies 4 calories per gram, so a day of eating can be converted from grams to calories and checked against the 10% to 35% range. Using the 2,000-calorie example from the page, 10% is 200 calories, or 50 grams, and 35% is 700 calories, or 175 grams. Your own calorie needs will differ.

## Putting it together

A few simple habits follow from the sources above. Add beans or lentils to a meal you already eat. Keep a small portion of nuts or seeds on hand and remember they are high in fat. Choose whole grains over refined ones. Spread your protein foods through the day instead of trying to fit them into one meal.
