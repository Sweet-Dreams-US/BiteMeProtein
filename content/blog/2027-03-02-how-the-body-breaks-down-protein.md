Protein shows up on food labels, in gym talk and in the names of snacks, but few people stop to ask what happens to it after they eat it. The answer is a series of steps in which the body takes apart what you ate and keeps the pieces it needs.

This post explains, in general terms, what protein is made of, how digestion breaks it down, and how health organizations describe the amount adults are advised to eat. It is general education, not advice for any one person.

## What protein is made of

MedlinePlus, the health information service of the U.S. National Library of Medicine, says that protein is a building block of life and that every cell in the human body contains it. Its basic structure is a chain of amino acids.

Think of amino acids as the individual parts that get linked together to form a protein. Harvard's Nutrition Source says protein is made from twenty-plus basic building blocks called amino acids. The kinds of amino acids and the order they are linked in give each protein its own makeup.

MedlinePlus adds that the body uses protein from food to repair cells and to make new ones.

## Step one: the stomach

When you eat a protein food such as eggs, yogurt or a baked good made with protein, digestion begins in the stomach. The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK), part of the National Institutes of Health, explains that glands in the stomach lining make stomach acid and enzymes that break down food, and its overview of digestive juices lists stomach acid and digestive enzymes among those that act on protein.

Stomach muscles mix the food with these juices. NIDDK says the stomach then slowly empties its contents, called chyme, into the small intestine.

## Step two: the pancreas and small intestine

The next steps happen in the small intestine, with help from the pancreas. NIDDK says the pancreas makes a digestive juice with enzymes that break down carbohydrates, fats and proteins, and sends it to the small intestine.

The small intestine also makes its own juice. NIDDK says that this juice, along with bile and pancreatic juice, completes the breakdown of proteins, carbohydrates and fats.

:::note Digestion ends in amino acids
MedlinePlus states that the protein in food is broken down into parts called amino acids during digestion.
:::

## Step three: absorption and use

Once protein has been broken into amino acids, the walls of the small intestine absorb the digested nutrients into the bloodstream. NIDDK says the blood carries amino acids, along with simple sugars, glycerol and some vitamins and salts, to the liver.

The liver stores, processes and delivers nutrients to the rest of the body when needed. NIDDK says the body then uses amino acids to build substances for energy, growth and cell repair. In other words, the protein in a meal is not used whole. It is taken apart, and the pieces are used where the body needs them.

## Essential, nonessential and conditional amino acids

MedlinePlus sorts amino acids into three groups. Essential amino acids are ones the body cannot make, so food has to supply them. They do not need to appear in every meal, and MedlinePlus says the balance over the whole day matters more. Nonessential amino acids are ones the body makes from essential amino acids or during the normal breakdown of protein. Conditional amino acids are needed during illness and stress.

Harvard's Nutrition Source names nine essential amino acids: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine.

## Complete and incomplete proteins

A complete protein, according to the Nutrition Source, contains all twenty-plus types of amino acids needed to make new protein in the body. It says animal foods such as meat, poultry, fish, eggs and dairy tend to be good sources of complete protein. Plant foods often lack one or more essential amino acids, so people who avoid animal foods are advised to eat a variety of plant proteins. Quinoa and chia seeds are named as complete plant proteins.

## How much protein adults are advised to eat

MedlinePlus says the recommended intake for healthy adults is 10% to 35% of total calorie needs. Protein provides 4 calories per gram. As its example, on a 2,000 calorie diet, 100 grams of protein is 400 calories, or 20% of daily calories. It also notes that one ounce (30 grams) of most protein rich foods has about 7 grams of protein.

Harvard's Nutrition Source reports that the National Academy of Medicine recommends a minimum of 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. That works out to just over 7 grams for every 20 pounds of body weight, or about 50 grams a day for a 140 pound person and about 70 grams for a 200 pound person. The Nutrition Source also says individual needs vary and that a registered dietitian can help work out an amount.

:::note Two ways to state the amount
MedlinePlus gives a share of calories, 10% to 35% for healthy adults. Harvard's Nutrition Source gives a minimum by body weight, 0.8 grams per kilogram per day, from the National Academy of Medicine.
:::

## Putting it together

Protein from food is broken down by stomach acid and enzymes, then by juices from the pancreas and small intestine, until it reaches the form of amino acids. Those amino acids are absorbed into the blood, carried to the liver, and used by the body for energy, growth and repair. Different foods supply different mixes of amino acids, and the amount an adult is advised to eat can be stated as a share of calories or as a minimum by body weight.
