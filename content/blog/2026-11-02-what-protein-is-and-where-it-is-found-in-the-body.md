Protein is printed on almost every food label, snack bar and menu board. Most people know it matters, but fewer can say what it actually is. This article covers the basics: what protein is made of, where it is found in your body, and why the food you eat has to supply it.

## What protein is

Protein is one of the three macronutrients, along with carbohydrate and fat. MedlinePlus, the health information service of the U.S. National Library of Medicine, describes proteins as the building blocks of life and notes that every cell in the human body contains protein.

Each protein is a chain of smaller units called amino acids. The Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health says proteins are built from more than twenty amino acids. The order of the amino acids in the chain, and the way the chain folds, decides what a given protein does.

:::note Worth knowing
Harvard's Nutrition Source states that at least 10,000 different proteins exist in the body. They all come from the same small set of amino acids.
:::

## Where protein is found in the body

Protein is not stored in one place. It is part of the body's structure. Harvard lists muscle, bone, skin and hair as places where protein is found. It also makes up enzymes, which drive chemical reactions in the body, and hemoglobin, the molecule in the blood that carries oxygen.

This is why protein shows up in so many conversations about food. Tissue is always being repaired and replaced, and the raw material comes from the amino acids in what a person eats. MedlinePlus says protein helps the body repair cells and make new ones, and that it is also important for growth and development in children, teens and pregnant women.

## Why food has to supply it

During digestion, the protein in food is broken down into amino acids. The body then uses those amino acids to build the proteins it needs. Harvard notes that the body stores little in the way of amino acids, so it must make them from scratch or modify others.

MedlinePlus sorts amino acids into three groups:

- Essential amino acids cannot be made by the body, so food has to provide them.
- Nonessential amino acids are made by the body from essential amino acids or during the normal breakdown of protein.
- Conditional amino acids are needed during illness and stress.

Harvard names nine essential amino acids: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine. MedlinePlus adds that these do not need to be eaten at every meal. The balance over the whole day matters more.

## Food sources of protein

MedlinePlus lists meat, milk, fish and eggs as animal sources, and soy, beans, legumes, nut butters and some grains, such as wheat germ and quinoa, as plant sources. It states plainly that you do not need animal products to get all the protein you need.

Harvard gives examples of protein per serving:

- Sirloin steak, broiled, 4 ounces: 33 grams
- Sockeye salmon, grilled, 4 ounces: 30 grams
- Chicken thigh without skin, 4 ounces: 28 grams
- Ham steak, 4 ounces: 22 grams
- Lentils, 1 cup cooked: 18 grams
- Milk, 8 ounces: 8 grams
- Almonds, dry roasted, 1 ounce: 6 grams

MedlinePlus gives a quick rule of thumb. One ounce (30 grams) of most protein-rich foods holds about 7 grams of protein. One large egg, a quarter cup of tofu and half a cup of cooked beans or lentils are in the same range.

## Complete and incomplete proteins

Harvard calls a protein complete when it contains all the essential amino acids the body needs. Animal foods such as meat, poultry, fish, eggs and dairy are generally complete. Most plant foods lack one or more essential amino acids, so people who eat a plant-based diet are advised to eat a variety of protein sources. Quinoa and chia seeds are plant foods that are complete.

:::note Worth knowing
Harvard's Nutrition Source says the "protein package" matters. The fats, fiber, sodium and other nutrients that come with the protein affect health more than the amount of protein alone.
:::

## How much protein people need

The National Academy of Medicine sets the minimum at 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, according to Harvard. That works out to just over 7 grams for every 20 pounds of body weight. Harvard gives about 50 grams per day for a 140-pound person and about 70 grams per day for a 200-pound person.

MedlinePlus states that healthy adults should get 10 percent to 35 percent of their calories from protein, and that each gram of protein provides 4 calories. On a 2,000 calorie diet, 100 grams of protein is 400 calories, or 20 percent. Needs vary with age, activity, health and diet, and children and teens may need different amounts.

## The short version

Protein is a chain of amino acids. It is part of muscle, bone, skin, hair, enzymes and hemoglobin. The body gets the essential amino acids it cannot make from food, and both animal and plant foods can supply them. For how much you should eat, the published figures are a starting point, and your own needs depend on your age, activity and health.
