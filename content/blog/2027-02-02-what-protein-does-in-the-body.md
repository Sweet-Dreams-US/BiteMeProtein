Protein shows up on food labels, in gym talk and in grocery store aisles, but it is easy to use the word without knowing what protein actually does. This article explains the job protein does in your body, how it is built, how much healthy adults are advised to eat, and where it comes from. The facts come from MedlinePlus, the health information site of the U.S. National Library of Medicine, and from the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health.

## What protein is made of

MedlinePlus says every cell in the body contains protein. Its basic structure is a chain of amino acids. Harvard says protein is built from more than twenty amino acids.

When you eat protein, your body breaks it down into amino acids during digestion. It then uses those amino acids to build the proteins it needs. In that sense, the protein in food is raw material for the protein in you.

## Repair and new cells

According to MedlinePlus, protein helps the body repair cells and make new ones. It is also important for growth and development in children, teens and pregnant women.

Harvard adds that protein is found throughout the body, including in muscle, bone, skin and hair. So when people say protein is a building block, they mean it literally. These tissues all contain it, and they all need a steady supply of amino acids to be maintained.

:::note Worth knowing
MedlinePlus says protein is important for growth and development in children, teens and pregnant women. This is general information. Questions about a child's or pregnant person's diet belong with their doctor or dietitian.
:::

## Enzymes and hemoglobin

Harvard points to two jobs that are easy to overlook. Protein makes the enzymes that drive many of the chemical reactions in the body. It also makes hemoglobin, the molecule in the blood that carries oxygen.

Neither of these shows up in the mirror the way muscle does, but both depend on getting enough amino acids from food.

## Essential, nonessential and conditional amino acids

MedlinePlus sorts amino acids into three groups.

- Essential amino acids cannot be made by the body, so they must come from food. Harvard says nine of them are essential.
- Nonessential amino acids are made by the body, either from essential amino acids or during normal protein breakdown.
- Conditional amino acids are needed during illness and stress.

MedlinePlus notes that essential amino acids do not need to be eaten at every meal. The balance over the whole day matters more.

## How much protein adults need

Harvard reports that the National Academy of Medicine sets the minimum at 0.8 grams of protein for every kilogram of body weight per day. That works out to roughly 7 grams for every 20 pounds of body weight.

MedlinePlus gives a different way to look at it. For healthy adults, protein should make up 10% to 35% of total calorie needs. Each gram of protein supplies 4 calories, so a 2,000 calorie diet could include about 100 grams of protein, which is 20% of calories. MedlinePlus adds that needs vary with age and overall calorie intake.

Both figures are guides for healthy adults, not personal prescriptions. If you have a medical condition, your own target may be different.

## Where protein comes from

MedlinePlus lists animal sources such as meat, fish, eggs and milk. Plant sources include soy, beans, lentils, nut butters, and grains such as quinoa and wheat germ. MedlinePlus says animal products are not required to meet protein needs.

Harvard says animal foods such as meat, poultry, fish, eggs and dairy are generally complete protein sources, meaning they supply the essential amino acids. Plant foods often lack one or more essential amino acids, so people who eat mostly plants should combine a variety of them over the day.

Harvard's guidance is to choose fish, poultry, beans and nuts, and to limit red meat and cheese. It also says processed meats should be avoided.

## Cautions worth knowing

Harvard raises a few points. Protein powders are not regulated by the FDA for safety, in the same way as other dietary supplements, so check labels for added sugars and calories. People with kidney or liver disease should monitor their protein intake under their physician's guidance. Some people have food allergies to protein sources such as eggs, milk, peanuts and soybeans.

:::note Worth knowing
Read ingredient lists on any packaged food, including baked goods, if you have an allergy. If you are unsure, ask the maker before you buy.
:::

## A simple way to use this

You do not need to count every gram to put this to use. Spread protein through the day, include a mix of sources, and read labels. If you eat mostly plants, vary what you choose so you cover the essential amino acids.

## The short version

Protein is a chain of amino acids. It repairs cells, helps build new ones, makes enzymes and carries oxygen as hemoglobin. Healthy adults are advised to eat at least 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight each day, from a mix of animal and plant foods. Check labels, and talk with your doctor if you have a condition that affects how much protein is right for you.
