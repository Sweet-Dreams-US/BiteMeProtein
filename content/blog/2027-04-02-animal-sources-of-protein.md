Protein shows up on nearly every food label and in nearly every conversation about eating well. Where it comes from matters as much as how much of it you get. This lesson looks at the animal sources of protein: meat, fish, poultry, eggs and dairy. It uses numbers from two named sources, MedlinePlus from the U.S. National Library of Medicine and the Nutrition Source at the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health.

## Why protein matters in the first place

MedlinePlus explains that the body cannot make some of the amino acids it needs, called essential amino acids, so they have to come from food. Animal foods supply them. The same page says you do not need to eat all of them at every meal, because the balance across the whole day matters more.

Protein also provides energy. MedlinePlus says it gives 4 calories per gram, and that healthy adults should get 10% to 35% of their total calories from protein. As an example, on a 2,000 calorie diet, 100 grams of protein is 400 calories, or 20% of the day's calories.

:::note Animal foods are not required
MedlinePlus states that animal products are not required to meet protein needs. Soy, beans, legumes and whole grains also supply amino acids. This lesson covers animal sources because they are one part of a bigger picture.
:::

## What counts as an animal source

MedlinePlus names meats, milk, fish and eggs, and also lists low fat dairy. Its examples of better choices include turkey or chicken with the skin removed, bison, lean cuts of beef or pork such as round, top sirloin or tenderloin with visible fat trimmed, and fish or shellfish.

A quick way to size a serving comes from the same page. One ounce of most protein rich foods, such as meat, fish or poultry, gives about 7 grams of protein. A 4 ounce portion therefore lands near 28 grams, which is close to the Harvard figures below.

## Fish and poultry

The Nutrition Source calls fish and poultry generally a top choice among animal proteins. Its examples show how much protein a 4 ounce portion carries:

- Grilled sockeye salmon has about 30 grams of protein, 1.1 grams of saturated fat and 104 milligrams of sodium. It also supplies about 1.0 gram of marine omega-3 fats.
- Chicken thigh without skin has about 28 grams of protein, 2.7 grams of saturated fat and 120 milligrams of sodium.

Harvard describes fatty fish as an excellent source of omega-3 fats, which it says are especially good for the heart. It also notes research linking skinless chicken to less weight gain over time and chicken with skin to more.

## Eggs

The Nutrition Source calls eggs a good choice and says animal foods like eggs are generally complete protein sources. That means they carry all of the essential amino acids. It also points out that eggs are among the common foods that can trigger allergic reactions, which matters for anyone cooking for a group.

## Dairy

Harvard recommends enjoying dairy in moderation, roughly 1 to 2 servings a day, and says yogurt is a better choice than milk or cheese. An 8 ounce glass of milk has about 8 grams of protein and 3.1 grams of saturated fat. In the studies it cites, regular cheese was linked to more weight gain, while low fat cheese and yogurt were linked to less.

## Red and processed meat

Red meat includes beef, pork, lamb, veal and goat. Harvard says it should be limited and kept for small portions or special occasions. A 4 ounce sirloin steak has about 33 grams of protein, which is more than salmon or chicken, but it also has about 4.6 grams of saturated fat.

The Nutrition Source reports associations from large studies. Each extra daily 3 ounce serving of unprocessed red meat was linked to a 13% higher risk of cardiovascular death, and each extra daily serving was linked to a 12% higher risk of type 2 diabetes. These are associations found in studies of groups of people, and they do not prove what will happen to any one person.

Processed meat is bacon, hot dogs, sausage and cold cuts, including poultry versions such as turkey bacon and deli chicken. Harvard says to avoid it. A 4 ounce ham steak has 22 grams of protein and 1.6 grams of saturated fat, but roughly 1,400 to 1,500 milligrams of sodium. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies processed meat as carcinogenic to humans, according to the Nutrition Source.

## The protein package

The most useful idea in the Harvard material is that protein arrives in a package. The fat, fiber, sodium and other parts that come with it may matter more for health than the grams of protein alone. The Nutrition Source says total protein as a share of calories was not linked to overall mortality, while the source of the protein mattered more.

Salmon, sirloin and ham show the tradeoffs. Sirloin gives high protein with more saturated fat. Ham is lower in fat but high in sodium. Salmon gives high protein with omega-3 fats and little sodium.

:::note Reading a label
When you compare foods, look at the protein number and at the saturated fat and sodium numbers next to it. The same 4 ounces can be a very different choice depending on the package.
:::

## Putting it together

For a quick summary, the sources agree on a few points. Fish, poultry, eggs and dairy all provide protein, and amounts per ounce are close for meat, fish and poultry. The package differs between them. Fish and skinless poultry come with less saturated fat than red meat, and processed meats bring the most sodium. Plant foods can meet protein needs without any animal products at all.

Your own needs depend on your age, health and activity. Use these numbers as a general guide when you read labels and plan meals.
